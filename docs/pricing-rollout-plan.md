# Myrtle365 — Talent & Venue Pricing + Rollout Plan

> **Status:** brainstorm / decision doc. Nothing here is built yet.
> Companion to [`social-and-payments-plan.md`](social-and-payments-plan.md), which locked
> **0% platform cut on tips**. That decision is what makes this document necessary:
> if we never touch the money flow, **subscriptions are the business model.**

---

## 0. The three constraints that decide everything

**1. We took 0% forever. That was the right call — and it moves the whole burden here.**
"100% of your tips go to you" is our best acquisition line in a market where every other
platform skims. Keep it, market it loudly, and never claw it back through a "tip processing
fee." The subscription has to carry the company.

**2. This is a seasonal market.** The Grand Strand is a summer economy. A working cover band
plays five nights a week in July and maybe two nights a month in January. A monthly
subscriber acquired in June is a churn event scheduled for October. **Every pricing decision
below is really a decision about how to survive winter.**

**3. It's a two-sided cold start.** Talent is numerous, price-sensitive, and is the inventory
that makes the site worth visiting. Venues are fewer, harder to sign, and have actual
marketing budgets. The standard resolution applies: **keep supply cheap, monetize the side
with budget.** Talent should never pay for a profile — they pay for *gigs*. Venues pay for a
*filled calendar*.

## 2. Annual discount — four options

The ask was specifically for pay-in-full options. Here's the menu, then the recommendation.

| | Term | Discount | Talent Pro | Venue Pro | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Two months free** | 12 mo | **16.7%** | $190 | $490 | ⭐ The standard, public offer |
| **B. Value-add annual** | 12 mo | 10% + perks | $205 + 1 featured week + 25 GB | $529 + 1 featured week + 50 GB | Margin-friendlier alternative to A |
| **C. Founding pre-sale** | 12 mo | **50% yr 1, price locked** | $114 | $294 | Time-boxed, first cohort only |
| **D. Season Pass** | 8 mo (Mar–Oct) | 10% | $137 | $353 | The seasonal reality; runs alongside A |
| *E. Quarterly* | 3 mo | 8% | $52 | $135 | For the hesitant; a bridge, not a headline |

**Recommendation: run A + D publicly, C as a time-boxed launch cohort.** Skip B unless
cash margin gets tight — perks read as weaker than money to a musician, though B is the
better deal for us since the perks cost us pennies.

### Why the annual discount is obviously worth it here

$190 ÷ $19 = **10 months**. Annual is accretive any time a monthly subscriber would have
churned before month 10. Same math on the venue side ($490 ÷ $49 = 10 months).

In a market that goes quiet from November through February, **almost nobody on monthly
reaches month 10.** Realistic monthly tenure for a gigging act here is 5–7 months. So:

- Monthly path: $19 × ~6 months = **~$114**, then they're gone and we re-acquire in spring.
- Annual path: **$190 today**, plus a renewal decision made *in season* when they're happy.

Annual isn't a discount we're absorbing. It's us buying out the churn cliff at a 17% price,
and getting the cash up front while we're pre-revenue. Push it hard.

### Why Season Pass matters more than it looks

Eight months at 10% off nets a **higher effective monthly rate** ($17.10) than annual
($15.83), and it's honest about the market instead of asking a beach musician to pay for
February. It converts the people who would otherwise say "I'll just do monthly and cancel in
the fall" — which is the single most common objection we should expect. It's also a
natural, non-salesy reason to contact every lapsed user each February.

## 4. Show Desk — "email us your schedule, we'll post it"

**The pitch:** talent and venues forward whatever they already have — a PDF from the booking
agent, a spreadsheet, a Facebook event, a photo of the chalkboard — to
`shows@myrtle365.com`. We enter every show on their page and their events feed. They never
log into a form.

This is probably the most valuable thing we can sell, and it's worth understanding why before
pricing it.

### Why it beats every other add-on

**Empty calendars are how marketplace listings die.** A venue page with no upcoming shows is
worth nothing to a fan, which means it's worth nothing to the venue, which means they churn.
Every feature in §1 assumes someone keeps the calendar current — and a bar owner at 11pm on a
Friday is not going to open a dashboard and key in twelve events.

**They already maintain the schedule somewhere.** In a spreadsheet, a printed calendar taped
behind the bar, a booking agent's PDF, a Facebook post. The work isn't creating the data — it's
retyping it. We're selling the removal of retyping, which is the only job here anyone
actually hates.

**It fixes the weak spot in §1.** As drafted, Talent Elite ($49) and Venue Premier ($149) were
justified by media limits and seats, which is thin — nobody upgrades for gigabytes. "We run
your calendar for you" is a real reason to pay $149. Making Show Desk the headline of the top
tier is the single best change to the packaging.

**Manual now, software later.** Concierge data entry is the standard cold-start play, and the
margin curve is friendly: sell it at service prices while a human does it, automate the parsing,
keep the price. See "the automation path" below.

### Pricing

| | **Talent** | **Venue** |
|---|---|---|
| Add-on to Pro | **$15/mo** ($150/yr) | **$39/mo** ($390/yr) |
| Included in | Touring (Elite), $49 | Marquee (Premier), $149 |
| Shows included / month | 15 | 30 |
| Overage | $1 / show | $1.50 / show |
| Turnaround | 1 business day | 1 business day; same-day on Marquee |

- **Requires Pro or above.** Not available on free — it keeps entitlement logic simple and it
  is the reason to leave the free tier.
- **The ladder is deliberate.** Venue Pro + Show Desk = $88 against Marquee at $149. Once a
  venue is already paying $88 for the thing they actually want, $149 for that plus everything
  else is an easy step up. Most Show Desk buyers should end up on the top tier, which is the
  point.
- **Volume caps are not optional.** See the economics below.

### The economics — why included volume has a number on it

Rough internal cost, and these are estimates that need a stopwatch on real emails before
anyone commits:

| | Per show | 20-show venue month |
|---|---|---|
| Entry, matching, dedupe, cleanup | ~2–3 min | ~50 min |
| Loaded cost at ~$22/hr | ~$0.80–1.10 | **~$18** |

So Venue Show Desk at $39 runs roughly 55% gross margin in the fully manual phase, and Talent
at $15 against ~8–15 shows runs about the same. That's a services margin, not a software
margin, and it only holds **if volume is capped**. One venue emailing a 60-show quarterly PDF
every week turns a $39 subscription into a loss. Hence included volume, overage, and a
fair-use clause.

Also note the demand curve is seasonal in the same direction as everything else: show volume
peaks in exactly the months we're busiest. Staffing for June–August needs a plan, though
seasonal part-time labor is one thing the Grand Strand has plenty of.

### How it actually works

The email address is the interface. Everything behind it needs designing, and most of the
failure modes are operational rather than technical:

1. **Verify the sender.** Match the From address to a verified account, or anyone can email in
   fake shows for a competitor's venue. Unknown senders get a reply-to-confirm, never a silent
   publish.
2. **Parse into a review queue.** Staff confirm every row before it goes live in the manual
   phase. Nothing auto-publishes on day one.
3. **Always reply with what we did.** "Added 14 shows through Sept 30 — here's your page. Two
   we couldn't read: [rows]." The confirmation email *is* the perceived value; without it
   they can't tell whether they're getting anything for the money.
4. **Handle updates with an explicit rule, or drown in duplicates.** This is the number one
   way this service breaks. Proposed rule: **each email replaces everything we have in the date
   range it covers.** Cancellations can also be sent as plain text — `CANCEL: Aug 22, The
   Tidewaters`. Say this in the onboarding email and repeat it in every confirmation footer.
5. **Ambiguity policy.** Booking-agent PDFs are messy: no year, "Sat 9pm" with no date, act
   names that don't match any profile ("Jimmy & the Boys" vs. "Jimmy and the Boys"). One
   clarifying reply, then enter best-effort and flag the row rather than stalling the batch.
6. **Publish the SLA and measure it.** "In your calendar within one business day" is the
   product. A flaky version of this is worse than not offering it.

### Meet them where they already are

The same back end should accept more than email, since the whole premise is not making people
change how they work:

- **Text a photo** of the chalkboard or printed calendar to a number. Bar managers will
  absolutely do this and will absolutely never open a web form.
- **Forward a Facebook event** — many venues already post there first.
- **Subscribe to their Google Calendar / iCal feed.** Highest leverage of all: zero ongoing
  effort from them, zero ongoing labor from us, and it self-updates. Any venue that already
  keeps a real calendar should be steered here, and it's cheap to build. Treat this as the
  endgame and email as the on-ramp.
- **A shared Google Sheet** on our template that we watch for changes.

### The automation path

This is what turns a services treadmill into a business:

| Phase | Who does the work | Time per email | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Manual | Human, internal queue | ~50 min / 20 shows | ~55% |
| 2. Assisted | LLM extracts, human reviews every row | ~15 min | ~80% |
| 3. Confident | Auto-publish high-confidence rows, human handles the rest | ~5 min | ~90% |

Document-to-structured-events extraction is very tractable now, and phase 1 generates exactly
the training and eval set we need — every messy real-world PDF, paired with the corrected
output a human produced. **The price does not change as this improves.** That's the whole
play: sell at service prices, migrate to software margins.

### Use it free before you sell it

The sharpest use of this isn't revenue — it's acquisition and cold-start.

**Give Show Desk away to every founding member during the Oct–Nov pre-sale (§5, phase 1).**
"Send us your fall schedule and we'll build your page" is a dramatically better sales pitch
than "sign up for our website," and it seeds the event data that makes the site worth visiting
before we ask a single fan to show up. It converts the sale and fills the database in one
motion.

Then it becomes a paid tier feature in February, and the founders who got it free are exactly
the accounts most likely to keep paying for it.

### What has to be built first

⚠️ **We have no events model at all.** README gap #5: `Tip.eventId` exists in the schema but
nothing populates it. Show Desk needs, in order:

1. An `Event` model and event pages (a prerequisite for §1's "events posted / month" limits
   too — those tiers can't ship without it either).
2. Auth and verified account emails, so senders can be matched.
3. An internal admin queue — inbox, parse preview, approve/edit/reject, reply. This is real
   product surface, not a shared mailbox someone checks.
4. Inbound email handling (Postmark or SES → webhook), attachment storage, and a dedupe key on
   `(venue, date, act)`.

None of this is exotic, but it is meaningfully more than "add a price to the pricing page." If
we want Show Desk live for the founding cohort in October, the Event model needs to start now.

## 5. Rollout timeline

Today is **August 2026** — the season is nearly over. Dropping a paywall now would ask people
to start paying exactly as their income stops. Don't. Use the off-season to build and pre-sell,
and switch on billing ahead of next season.

| Phase | When | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Instrument** | Aug–Sep 2026 | Everything stays free. Build the entitlements layer and usage metering. **Start the `Event` model now** — Show Desk and the tier event limits both block on it (§4). Capture end-of-season activity data *while venues still care*. Interview 15 talent + 10 venues on price. |
| **1. Founding pre-sale** | Oct–Nov 2026 | Offer **C** — annual only, 50% off year one, price locked for life while continuous. Cap it: **first 100 talent, first 25 venues.** Lead the pitch with **free Show Desk** — "send us your fall schedule and we'll build your page" (§4). Off-season is when venues plan next summer's bookings, so this is when it lands. Real cash, real validation, and the event data seeded in one motion. |
| **2. Build & publish** | Dec 2026–Jan 2027 | Ship add-ons and metering. Onboard founders. **Publish the public price list with "list prices effective March 1"** — advance notice converts fence-sitters into founders. |
| **3. Paywall on** | Feb 2027 | New signups hit the paid tiers. **Show Desk becomes paid** — founders who had it free are the likeliest converts. Every existing user gets a **90-day Pro trial ending May 31**, so they experience Pro through the start of peak season and decide when they're making money. |
| **4. Season Pass** | Mar 2027 | Option **D** goes on sale as the season opens. Peak season is peak willingness to pay. |
| **5. Metered upsells** | Jun 2027 | Turn on broadcasting packs and overage once the base subscription is stable. Don't debug billing and metering at the same time. |
| **6. First price review** | Sep 2027 | Venue Pro $49 → $79 if the calendar-fill data supports it. Founders grandfathered, 60 days notice, existing annual terms honored. |

## 6. Policies to decide before launch

- **Pause, don't cancel.** Monthly subscribers can pause up to **4 months per year** — profile
  stays live, paid features switch off, $0 charged. This is the single highest-leverage
  anti-churn feature for a seasonal market, and it's cheaper than winning them back in spring.
- **Grandfathering.** Founding prices hold as long as the subscription is continuous; one
  30-day lapse grace. Say this in writing — it's most of why founders buy.
- **Refunds.** 30-day full refund on annual, prorated credit after. Cheaper than chargebacks
  and much cheaper than the reputation hit in a small market where everyone knows everyone.
- **Annual renewal notice at T-30 and T-7.** Surprise $490 charges generate disputes.
- **Price increases:** at most once a year, 60 days notice, in-term annual plans honored.
- **Never gate:** the profile itself, the tip jar, the QR, the 0% promise, or basic presence in
  search. Paywalling a musician out of getting tipped would be self-defeating.

## 7. What this is worth (illustrative)

Rough sizing — **these penetration and market-size figures are estimates for shaping the
model, not researched numbers.** Validate in Phase 0 before anyone plans around them.

Assume ~900 gigging acts and ~250 live-music venues on the Grand Strand.

| Segment | Paid | Mix | MRR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Talent (12% of 900 = 108) | 108 | 75% Pro / 25% Elite | $2,862 |
| Venue (20% of 250 = 50) | 50 | 80% Pro / 20% Premier | $3,450 |
| Add-ons | ~18% attach | | ~$900 |
| **Total** | | | **~$7,200 MRR ≈ $86k ARR** |

**Show Desk moves this line more than any other add-on.** At a 35% attach on paid venues and
15% on paid talent, plus the accounts it pulls up into the top tiers, it plausibly adds
**$1,000–1,400 MRR (~$12–17k ARR)** — but at roughly 55% gross margin in the manual phase
rather than software margin, so it contributes materially less to the bottom line than the
same revenue from a subscription. Model it separately, not as another 18%-attach add-on.

That's a credible year one for a single-market marketplace, and it tells us something
important: **subscriptions alone cap out fast in one metro.** The path past ~$100k ARR is
(a) promotion/featured inventory, (b) booking fees once venue→talent booking is real, and
(c) a second market — Charleston, Wilmington, Savannah. Price the subscription to maximize
*density*, not ARPU, because density is what makes (a) and (b) possible.

## 8. Risks

1. **Pay-to-win ranking destroys fan trust.** If paid talent outranks better free talent,
   discovery gets worse and fans stop using us — which destroys the thing venues are paying
   for. **Keep organic ranking quality-weighted.** Paid unlocks *eligibility* for boosts, and
   every boosted slot is visibly labeled "Featured."
2. **Broadcasting COGS.** Metered from day one, or it eats the margin. (§3)
3. **Show Desk turns into an unpaid treadmill.** It's a labor business until the parsing is
   automated. Capped volume, overage, a published SLA, and a real commitment to phases 2–3 of
   the automation path are what keep it from quietly consuming the team. If we can't staff it
   reliably through a Grand Strand summer, don't sell it — a missed calendar is worse than no
   calendar. (§4)
4. **Paywalling supply too early.** If we charge talent before venues are actively booking
   through the site, Pro has nothing to sell. Watch booking-request volume as the gate for
   Phase 3, not the calendar.
5. **Seasonal churn.** Mitigated by annual, Season Pass, and pause. (§2, §5)
6. **Sales tax.** South Carolina's treatment of SaaS/digital services needs a real answer from
   an accountant before we take the first dollar. Flagging, not advising.
7. **Billing scope creep.** Subscriptions are **Stripe Billing** and are entirely separate
   from tips. Tips stay handle-handoff, Model A, 0%, and Myrtle365 stays out of the money
   flow. Taking card payments for *our own* subscription does not make us a money transmitter
   for *tips* — but keep the two systems visibly separate in code and in copy so nobody
   confuses them.

- **One entitlements module** — `src/lib/entitlements.ts`, pure and unit-tested like

- **Soft limits, not hard walls.** Over quota → "you're over, here's the pack" — never delete
  media, never cut a live stream mid-set.
- Requires auth first (README known gap #1). Nothing here is buildable until there's a
  signed-in user to bill.
